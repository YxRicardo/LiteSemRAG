\title{LiteSemRAG: Lightweight Semantic-Aware Graph Retrieval for Robust RAG}

\author{}


\maketitle

\begin{abstract}

Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) can improve evidence aggregation, but existing graph RAG systems often depend on large language models (LLMs) for both index construction and query-time reasoning, which increases token cost, latency, and deployment complexity. We propose \textbf{LiteSemRAG}, a lightweight semantic-aware graph retrieval framework that preserves contextual semantic matching while restricting generative LLM use to bounded indexing-time semantic-label preparation. LiteSemRAG builds a heterogeneous semantic graph that separates surface lexical units, context-dependent semantic nodes, and text chunks, allowing polysemous words and phrases to be indexed by semantics rather than by surface form alone. Its semantic nodes are constructed from contextual span embedding representations with anchor-guided semantic assignment, and ambiguous nodes can be linked to human-readable sense descriptions for inspection. During retrieval, LiteSemRAG resolves query units to semantic nodes and ranks chunks by combining direct semantic relevance with locally supported semantic co-occurrence. Retrieval-only experiments on HotpotQA, SciFact, and FEVER show that LiteSemRAG achieves competitive retrieval accuracy across most reported metrics compared with representative recent graph RAG baselines, while reducing indexing latency and eliminating query-time generative-LLM token consumption.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020retrieval,borgeaud2022improving,izacard2021leveraging,zhao2026retrieval} has become a fundamental paradigm for improving factual grounding and knowledge integration in large language models (LLMs) \cite{brown2020language,devlin2019bert}.  By augmenting generation with retrieval over external corpora, RAG systems can improve factuality, adaptability, and domain generalization in knowledge-intensive tasks \cite{guu2020retrieval}. Therefore, the quality and efficiency of the retrieval component have become critical factors in RAG systems. Early RAG systems mainly rely on flat chunk-level retrieval using dense or sparse representations~\cite{karpukhin2020dense,khattab2020colbert}. However, RAG systems with flat chunk-level retrieval often struggle with document structure, semantic variation, and multi-hop evidence dependencies. To address these limitations, recent work has introduced structure-aware and graph-based retrieval frameworks \cite{sarthi2024raptor,gutierrez2024hipporag,edge2024local,guo2024lightrag}. They construct hierarchical or graph-based index structures to enable multi-level reasoning and structured evidence aggregation. These approaches demonstrate that structured indexing can substantially improve retrieval effectiveness, particularly in complex reasoning scenarios. However, many existing graph-based RAG frameworks heavily rely on LLMs during both indexing and querying. Although LLMs provide powerful capabilities for semantic extraction, summarization, and reasoning, utilization of LLMs also introduces substantial computational overhead, high token consumption, latency variability, and sensitivity to the specific underlying model. These factors can significantly limit the practicality of such systems in resource-constrained or large-scale scenarios. NoLLMRAG~\cite{yunollmrag} is among the first systems to eliminate LLM dependence in graph-based RAG. However, it only relies on surface-level lexical and static statistical structures, which limits contextual and semantic understanding.

In this paper, we propose \textbf{LiteSemRAG}, a lightweight and LLM-frugal semantic-aware graph retrieval framework. Unlike existing graph-based RAG frameworks that depend on LLMs for all semantic extraction and reasoning, LiteSemRAG demonstrates that strong semantic-aware indexing and retrieval can be achieved without making LLMs the recurring engine for graph construction or query-time reasoning. LiteSemRAG does not use generative LLMs during query-time retrieval; during indexing, generative LLM calls are restricted to selecting semantic candidates and labeling semantic anchors. LiteSemRAG constructs a heterogeneous semantic graph directly by exploiting contextual embeddings. The heterogeneous semantic graph explicitly separates surface lexical representations (token nodes) from context-dependent semantic meanings (semantic nodes), enabling dynamic modeling of polysemy while avoiding LLM-centered graph construction. The index also retains multiword expressions as lexical evidence, helping to preserve phrase-level precision while modeling contextual meanings. To enhance robustness, LiteSemRAG applies an anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm to words and phrases that appear with multiple contextual meanings. The assignment algorithm samples a small anchor set with farthest-first traversal and random coverage, propagates anchor labels over a mutual-kNN occurrence graph, uses cross-encoder prediction scores to veto unreliable neighbor overrides, and resolves remaining uncertain occurrences with cross-encoder fallback. These bounded indexing-time LLM calls support semantic-label preparation: candidate sense descriptions are merged and filtered to remove redundancy and noise, and selected anchors are labeled to seed propagation. At the query stage, LiteSemRAG resolves query units through exact, partial, and embedding-based semantic-node matching, and ranks chunks by combining direct semantic evidence with bounded pairwise co-occurrence support. This design keeps retrieval as a deterministic encoder-driven process while preserving both contextual semantic disambiguation and lexical precision.
We evaluate LiteSemRAG on three widely used benchmarks: HotpotQA, SciFact, and FEVER. Experimental results show that LiteSemRAG achieves the best Recall@10 on HotpotQA and FEVER, the best MRR@10 on SciFact and FEVER among ranked graph RAG baselines, and substantially lower query-time cost. This design makes LiteSemRAG particularly suitable for large-scale retrieval systems where LLM-based indexing introduces prohibitive computational and monetary costs.
The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a lightweight and LLM-frugal semantic-aware graph retrieval framework that explicitly models polysemy through contextual embeddings.
    \item We introduce an anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm, combining farthest-first anchor sampling, mutual-kNN label propagation, cross-encoder margin veto, and cross-encoder fallback to effectively assign semantics.
    \item We design a group-aware semantic co-occurrence retrieval strategy that combines semantic-node matching, direct semantic evidence, and bounded pairwise chunk support.
    \item We demonstrate that LiteSemRAG achieves competitive retrieval performance on most reported metrics compared to LLM-based graph RAG systems while significantly improving efficiency through targeted indexing-time LLM use and encoder-driven retrieval.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) \cite{lewis2020retrieval} combines a parametric generator with a non-parametric retriever on an external corpus. RAG has become a core component for improving performance in knowledge-intensive natural language processing tasks. However, many RAG frameworks still rely on flat chunk retrieval, which can struggle to capture document structure, semantic variation, and multi-hop evidence dependencies \cite{karpukhin2020dense}. Several studies have improved the retrieval component through dense or sparse neural representations. Karpukhin et al. \cite{karpukhin2020dense} proposed a dual-encoder dense retriever that maps questions and passages into a shared embedding space for nearest-neighbor search. ColBERT \cite{khattab2020colbert} is proposed by exploiting late interaction to preserve token-level matching signals while remaining efficient for large-scale retrieval. These methods substantially improve retrieval quality, but they generally retrieve from flat format collections and do not explicitly induce graph-structured semantic units.

\subsection{Structure-Aware and Graph-Based RAG}
Recent work has explored structured retrieval to better support long-context reasoning and multi-hop evidence aggregation. RAPTOR \cite{sarthi2024raptor} builds a hierarchical tree of clustered and summarized text units, enabling retrieval at multiple abstraction levels. GraphRAG \cite{edge2024local} constructs an entity-centric graph and uses LLM-based extraction and community summaries to support global and local query answering. HippoRAG \cite{gutierrez2024hipporag} combines knowledge graphs with personalized PageRank to improve multi-hop retrieval efficiency. LightRAG \cite{guo2024lightrag} further simplifies graph-based RAG with dual-level retrieval over low-level and high-level knowledge structures. These methods show that structured indexing can improve retrieval quality beyond flat chunk search, especially for complex reasoning tasks. However, the dominant graph-based RAG frameworks still depend on LLMs during indexing and querying, which increases latency, cost, and reproducibility sensitivity to the underlying model. NoLLMRAG \cite{yunollmrag} is the first graph-based RAG framework that eliminates reliance on LLMs during indexing and querying. However, due to absence of explicit semantic modeling, it only relies on surface-level lexical and
static statistical structures, which can significantly limit its effectiveness in complex or multi-hop scenarios.

\section{Approach}
LiteSemRAG is a lightweight semantic retrieval framework that constructs a heterogeneous semantic graph to preserve contextual semantic meaning while minimizing dependence on generative LLM calls. The framework consists of two main components: \textbf{Heterogeneous Semantic Graph Index}, where semantic-aware structures are derived primarily from contextual token-level embeddings, and \textbf{Semantic Co-occurrence Retrieval}, where query spans are matched to semantic nodes and chunks are ranked by a unified evidence score. LiteSemRAG first analyzes the query with the same phrase-aware representation used during indexing, resolves each query unit to exact, partial, or embedding-based semantic-node matches, and then ranks candidate chunks by combining direct semantic evidence with bounded pairwise co-occurrence support. This design gives every matched semantic node a direct retrieval path while using co-occurrence evidence as a controlled score boost.
We define a multi-layer heterogeneous semantic graph as $G = (V, E)$, where the node set is defined as $V = D \cup C \cup S \cup T$. The node types are defined as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Document nodes} ($D$): each represents a document in the corpus.
    \item \textbf{Chunk nodes} ($C$): each represents a text chunk derived from a document.
    \item \textbf{Semantic nodes} ($S$): each represents a specific semantic meaning of a token node.
    \item \textbf{Token nodes} ($T$): each represents a unique surface lexical unit. A token node may correspond to a single token, a lexicalized entity or a phrase.
\end{itemize}
The edge set $E$ consists of bidirectional edges of three types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Document--Chunk edges: indicating that a chunk belongs to a document.
    \item Chunk--Semantic edges: indicating that a semantic meaning appears in a chunk.
    \item Semantic--Token edges: linking each semantic node to its corresponding surface lexical unit.
\end{itemize}

The overall workflow of LiteSemRAG proceeds in two stages. During indexing, documents are split into chunks, important spans are extracted and routed into lexical or compositional units, contextual span embeddings are collected, and semantic nodes are constructed for ambiguous units through anchor-guided semantic assignment. During retrieval, a query is converted into the same phrase-aware units, matched to semantic nodes through lexical and embedding-based routes, and ranked by combining direct semantic evidence with bounded local co-occurrence support.

Given a document collection, LiteSemRAG first splits each document into chunks and extracts important textual spans, such as entities, phrases, and tokens, using NLP tools. Each extracted span is represented as a surface lexical unit, which serves as the entry point for indexing and semantic matching. LiteSemRAG then applies a token-level text encoder to each chunk and obtains contextual embeddings for all span occurrences within the chunk. Since a single encoder pass generates contextual representations for multiple spans simultaneously, this step introduces limited indexing overhead. After collecting contextual embeddings across the corpus, LiteSemRAG constructs semantic nodes by analyzing the embeddings of span occurrences and organizes documents, chunks, semantic nodes, and token nodes into a heterogeneous graph. This design separates surface lexical format from context-dependent semantics: token nodes maintain surface-level forms, semantic nodes capture distinct semantic interpretations, and chunk connections indicate where each semantic appears in the corpus.


\subsubsection{Phrase-Aware Lexical Routing}
Multiword phrases are not always suitable as independent semantic units. Some phrases are lexicalized expressions whose meanings depend on the complete phrase, while others are compositional expressions in which the head carries the main semantic type and the modifiers provide additional constraints. LiteSemRAG distinguishes these two cases during indexing. Lexicalized expressions and named entities, such as \emph{Machine Learning}, are preserved as complete phrase anchors, and semantic discovery is performed over the full phrase. For compositional phrases, LiteSemRAG builds a phrase--head relation instead of treating the full phrase only as an isolated unit. For example, \emph{American director} is represented by the full surface phrase \emph{American director}, the semantic head \emph{director}, and the modifier \emph{American}. The full phrase preserves exact surface evidence, while the head enables semantically related phrases, such as \emph{French director} and \emph{American director}, to share an aggregation route. The modifier is retained as occurrence-level evidence and can be used as a query-time constraint. This design preserves phrase-level evidence for interpretation and retrieval, while allowing semantic-node construction to aggregate related occurrences through compact lexical units such as phrase heads.


\subsubsection{Dynamic semantic node creation}

A key component of LiteSemRAG is the dynamic construction of semantic nodes from contextual span embeddings. Instead of assigning a fixed representation to each surface lexical unit, LiteSemRAG creates distinct semantic nodes to represent its context-dependent meanings. This allows the model to explicitly capture polysemy without relying on LLM-based parsing. For each surface lexical unit $t$, LiteSemRAG collects all of its contextual occurrences. Each occurrence records the chunk in which the unit appears, the corresponding evidence span, modifier information when applicable, and the local context surrounding the span. We denote the occurrence set of $t$ as
$
O_t = \{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_n\}.
$
For each occurrence $o_i$, LiteSemRAG stores a normalized contextual span embedding $e_i$, which represents the semantic position of $t$ in that specific context. Let
$
E_t = \{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_n\}
$
denote the set of contextual span embeddings for $t$. To determine whether a surface lexical unit may have multiple meanings, LiteSemRAG measures the concentration of its embedding distribution using the dispersion score, denoted as S-mean:
\[
S\text{-mean}(t) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \cos(e_i, \bar{e}_t),
\]
where
$\bar{e}_t = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i$
is the mean embedding of all occurrences of $t$. A high S-mean indicates that the occurrences are concentrated around a single semantic direction, suggesting that the unit is likely single-semantic. In contrast, a low S-mean suggests that the occurrences may form multiple contextual semantic regions. To avoid unnecessary semantic splitting for frequent but uninformative spans, LiteSemRAG also considers inverse document frequency. Semantic discovery is triggered for a surface lexical unit $t$ only when
$
IDF(t) > \tau_{\text{idf}}
\quad \text{and} \quad
S\text{-mean}(t) < \tau_{\text{disp}},
$
where $\tau_{\text{idf}}$ and $\tau_{\text{disp}}$ are predefined thresholds. If this condition is not satisfied, $t$ is treated as single-semantic, and LiteSemRAG creates one semantic node by averaging all of its contextual span embeddings. Contextual span embeddings are useful for identifying spans that are unlikely to be polysemous. If the embeddings of a surface lexical unit are highly concentrated, the span is likely to have a single dominant meaning, and LiteSemRAG can safely avoid further semantic discovery.

However, dispersed embeddings do not necessarily indicate multiple meanings. Contextual embeddings encode not only the core semantics of a span, but also surrounding topics, syntactic roles, neighboring entities, and sentence-level discourse information. Therefore, occurrences with the same meaning may still form different embedding regions due to contextual variation. Our experiments show that clustering algorithms such as HDBSCAN can produce multiple embedding clusters that do not correspond to distinct semantic meanings. These clusters may reflect topical or syntactic differences rather than true polysemy. Therefore, LiteSemRAG uses embedding dispersion only as a trigger for further analysis, rather than directly treating embedding clusters as semantic nodes. More reliable semantic assignment is then performed through the anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm.


\subsubsection{Anchor-guided Semantic Assignment Algorithm}
When the S-mean criterion indicates that a surface lexical unit may be polysemous, LiteSemRAG assigns semantic meanings to its occurrences using an anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm. The algorithm avoids both geometry-only embedding clustering and exhaustive semantic judgment over every occurrence. Instead, a small set of explicitly judged anchors provides semantic supervision, a mutual-kNN graph propagates labels through locally coherent embedding neighborhoods, and a cross-encoder verifies or resolves cases where propagation is uncertain. In this way, semantic-node construction is formulated as an occurrence-level assignment problem over $O_t$.

LiteSemRAG first selects a small anchor set $A_t$ from the occurrence set $O_t$. Anchors are selected by combining \emph{farthest-first traversal} (FFT) sampling with random sampling from the remaining non-selected occurrences according to a predefined ratio. Starting from an initial occurrence, FFT repeatedly selects the occurrence whose embedding is farthest from its nearest selected anchor:
\[
o^* = \arg\max_{o_i \in O_t \setminus A_t} \min_{o_j \in A_t} d(e_i,e_j),
\]
where
$
d(e_i,e_j)=1-\cos(e_i,e_j).
$
FFT encourages the anchor set to cover boundary and minority regions in the embedding space, while random supplementation reduces deterministic sampling bias and allows dense regions to contribute anchors.

For the target surface lexical unit $t$, LiteSemRAG retrieves a set of candidate meanings
$
M_t = \{m_1,m_2,\dots,m_r\}
$
from an external knowledge source according to its surface form of $t$. The raw candidate list is then merged and filtered with LLM assistance to remove redundant or noisy descriptions before semantic assignment. This preparation step is separated from occurrence-level scoring because candidate lists often contain overlapping entities, alias-driven duplicates, overly broad definitions, and descriptions that require semantic rewriting or consolidation.

Each retained candidate meaning is represented by a textual description. For every anchor occurrence $o_i \in A_t$, the LLM assistant compares the local occurrence context with the retained candidate meanings and assigns the most appropriate semantic label. Since candidate meanings are retrieved from an external knowledge source and consolidated into explicit textual descriptions, each assigned label is associated with a human-readable meaning description. This improves the explainability and readability of the resulting semantic nodes.

After anchor labeling, LiteSemRAG first checks whether the anchors exhibit a strong semantic consensus. If the number of anchors assigned to a semantic label reaches the consensus threshold, LiteSemRAG treats the surface lexical unit as single-semantic within the current corpus and assigns the majority anchor label to all occurrences of $t$. This early exit avoids unnecessary semantic splitting when the anchor evidence suggests that nearly all observed contexts share the same meaning.

If no strong consensus is observed, LiteSemRAG builds a mutual-kNN graph over the normalized occurrence embeddings. Let $\mathcal{N}_k(i)$ denote the $k$ nearest neighbors of occurrence $o_i$ under cosine distance. An undirected edge is created only when the neighbor relation is reciprocal:
\[
i \sim j \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
j \in \mathcal{N}_k(i) \;\; \text{and} \;\; i \in \mathcal{N}_k(j).
\]
This mutual-neighbor constraint removes many asymmetric edges caused by hubs or sparse boundary points, making label propagation more conservative than standard kNN voting.

Propagation proceeds iteratively from the labeled anchors. For an unlabeled occurrence $o_u$, LiteSemRAG collects the labels of its currently labeled mutual-kNN neighbors. If the majority label is $y$, its vote ratio is defined as
\[
\rho_u(y)=
\frac{|\{o_j \in \mathcal{N}^{\text{mut}}_k(u): y_j=y\}|}
{|\{o_j \in \mathcal{N}^{\text{mut}}_k(u): y_j \text{ is assigned}\}|}.
\]
The label $y$ is propagated to $o_u$ only when $\rho_u(y)$ exceeds a predefined vote threshold. This procedure expands anchor labels through locally coherent neighborhoods without requiring semantic judgment for every easy occurrence.

To prevent propagation from overriding strong candidate-level evidence, LiteSemRAG further introduces a margin-based veto strategy using an additional cross-encoder, which is separate from the token-level text encoder used to generate contextual embeddings. The cross-encoder scores each occurrence against every candidate semantic meaning. Let $y^{\text{CE}}_u$ be the cross-encoder top-1 label for occurrence $o_u$, and define the top-1 margin as
\[
\Delta_u =
f_P(o_u,y^{\text{CE}}_u)
-
\max_{y \neq y^{\text{CE}}_u} f_P(o_u,y),
\]
where $f_P(o_u,y)$ denotes the cross-encoder score between occurrence $o_u$ and semantic label $y$. If neighbor voting proposes a label that conflicts with $y^{\text{CE}}_u$, and the cross-encoder margin is sufficiently high, the veto blocks the propagation decision. In contrast, when the cross-encoder margin is low, LiteSemRAG allows locally consistent neighbor evidence to override the cross-encoder top-1 prediction. Thus, graph propagation is mainly used for uncertain cases, while confident candidate-level semantic judgments are preserved.

After propagation stops, any remaining uncertain occurrences are resolved using either the cross-encoder or an LLM fallback. These cases may occur when an occurrence has no reliable labeled mutual neighbors, when its vote ratio is too weak, or when the cross-encoder veto blocks the propagated label. Once every occurrence has been assigned a semantic label, LiteSemRAG creates one semantic node for each retained meaning. The embedding of semantic node $s_y$ is computed by averaging the contextual embeddings of all occurrences assigned to meaning $y$:
\[
e_{s_y} = |O_y|^{-1} \sum_{o_i \in O_y} e_i,
\]
where $O_y$ denotes the set of occurrences assigned to $y$. Chunk nodes are then connected to the corresponding semantic nodes according to the occurrence-level assignments, while the original token node remains connected to all of its semantic nodes as the shared surface lexical unit.

\subsubsection{Design Rationale}
The heterogeneous semantic graph is designed to balance lexical precision and contextual semantic retrieval. Token nodes preserve exact surface forms, while semantic nodes represent semantic meanings derived from contextual span occurrences. This separation avoids forcing all occurrences of the same word or phrase into one representation, but also prevents the index from losing exact lexical evidence in a purely embedding-based retrieval space. S-mean and IDF first filter out concentrated or uninformative units, and anchor-guided assignment is applied only to likely ambiguous cases. By using sparse anchors, mutual-kNN propagation, and cross-encoder verification, the index treats embedding geometry as local evidence rather than as the final sense boundary. As a result, LiteSemRAG obtains inspectable semantic nodes with limited LLM involvement and supports semantic-node retrieval without LLM-centered graph construction.

\subsection{Semantic Co-occurrence Retrieval}

During the query stage, LiteSemRAG exploits the phrase-aware index and ranks chunks with a unified semantic evidence score. The goal is to preserve lexical precision for exact phrase evidence, use semantic nodes to resolve context-dependent meanings, and reward chunks in which multiple query-relevant meanings provide local support for each other. Given a query, LiteSemRAG first constructs query units, maps them to semantic-node candidates, computes direct chunk evidence, and then applies a bounded co-occurrence boost over semantic-node pairs.

\subsubsection{Composition-Aware Query Unit Construction}

For lexicalized query spans, the query unit is kept as the complete surface phrase. For compositional query spans, LiteSemRAG constructs a query group consisting of the full phrase, its semantic head, and its modifier units. For example, the query span \emph{American director} is represented by the full-phrase unit \emph{American director}, the head unit \emph{director}, and the modifier unit \emph{American}. The full phrase preserves exact lexical evidence when the same phrase appears in the index. The head unit enables aggregation over semantically related occurrences that share the same head, while the modifier unit provides an auxiliary constraint for distinguishing different surface realizations under the same head.

Let a compositional query group be denoted as
\[
g_q = (p_q,\; h_q,\; M_q),
\]
where $p_q$ is the full phrase, $h_q$ is the semantic head, and $M_q$ is the set of modifier units. Each member of the group is assigned a role weight $\beta_r$, where
\[
\beta_{\mathrm{phrase}} > \beta_{\mathrm{head}} > \beta_{\mathrm{modifier}}.
\]
Standalone query tokens whose spans are already covered by a compositional phrase are not treated as independent query targets. This avoids double-counting the same evidence while still allowing the full phrase, head, and modifiers to contribute through the shared query group.

\subsubsection{Multi-Level Semantic Matching}

For each query unit $u_q$ with contextual embedding $e_q$, LiteSemRAG retrieves candidate \textbf{semantic nodes} rather than only matching surface token nodes. The matching process uses three complementary routes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exact match:} semantic nodes linked to the indexed surface lexical unit whose surface form matches $u_q$.

    \item \textbf{Partial match:} semantic nodes linked to indexed phrase anchors whose surface forms contain all words in $u_q$.

    \item \textbf{Similarity match:} semantic nodes whose embeddings are closest to $e_q$ when lexical matching does not provide sufficient candidates.
\end{itemize}

For exact and partial matches, if the matched surface lexical unit has multiple semantic nodes, LiteSemRAG selects the semantic node most consistent with the query context:
\[
s^*(t) = \arg\max_{s \in S(t)} \cos(e_q, e_s),
\]
where $S(t)$ denotes the semantic nodes linked to surface lexical unit $t$, and $e_s$ is the embedding of semantic node $s$. Similarity matching is used as a fallback over the semantic-node embedding space when exact and partial lexical matching return insufficient candidates.

The matching process produces a set of matched semantic nodes $S_q$. Each matched semantic node is assigned an effective query weight $\eta_s$ according to its matching route. Exact matches receive full weight, partial matches receive a length-normalized weight $|u_q|/|t|$, where $|\cdot|$ counts tokenized words, and similarity-only matches receive a cosine-based weight with an additional penalty factor. For compositional query groups, this route-level weight is further multiplied by the role weight of the corresponding phrase, head, or modifier unit.

\subsubsection{Direct Semantic Evidence}

The first component of chunk scoring measures how strongly a candidate chunk directly supports the matched query semantics. For a semantic node $s \in S_q$ and a chunk $c$, LiteSemRAG uses a semantic BM25-style contribution:
\[
\mathrm{BM25}(s,c) =
G(s)
\cdot
\frac{f(s,c)(k_1+1)}
{f(s,c)+k_1\left(1-b+b\frac{|c|}{\mathrm{avgcl}}\right)},
\]
where $G(s)$ is the chunk-level discriminative weight of semantic node $s$, $f(s,c)$ is the frequency of $s$ in chunk $c$, $|c|$ is the chunk length, and $\mathrm{avgcl}$ is the average chunk length. The base semantic evidence score of chunk $c$ is
\[
\mathrm{Base}(c)
=
\sum_{s \in S_q(c)}
\eta_s \cdot \mathrm{BM25}(s,c),
\]
where $S_q(c)$ is the set of matched semantic nodes that appear in chunk $c$. This score provides a direct retrieval path for every matched semantic node, including nodes that do not co-occur with other query nodes.

For compositional query groups, LiteSemRAG applies a group-aware aggregation rule. If a chunk contains full-phrase semantic evidence for a group, the phrase contribution is used as the primary group contribution, and the corresponding head or modifier evidence from the same group is not counted again. If the full phrase is absent, the head and modifier units can jointly contribute according to their role weights. This rule reflects the asymmetric importance of phrase, head, and modifier evidence: exact phrase evidence is the strongest signal, head evidence supports semantic aggregation, and modifier evidence refines the intended surface realization.

\subsubsection{Bounded Pairwise Co-occurrence Boosting}

The second component of chunk scoring rewards chunks in which multiple matched semantic nodes locally support each other. For semantic nodes $i$ and $j$, LiteSemRAG defines a corpus-level co-occurrence strength:
\[
w_{ij}
=
\frac{|C(i) \cap C(j)|}
{\sqrt{|C(i)| \cdot |C(j)|}},
\]
where $C(i)$ denotes the set of chunks containing semantic node $i$. This normalized overlap reduces the influence of globally frequent semantics while emphasizing pairs that co-occur consistently across the corpus.

For a candidate chunk $c$, a semantic-node pair contributes a local boost only when both endpoints appear in $c$. LiteSemRAG further distinguishes stronger sentence-level support from weaker chunk-level support:
\[ \ell_c(i,j) = \begin{cases} \ell_{\text{sent}}, & \text{same sentence in } c,\\ \ell_{\text{chunk}}, & \text{same chunk but different sentences}. \end{cases} \]
Pairs formed only by different members of the same compositional query group are excluded from this boost, because their phrase-level relation is already handled by direct group-aware evidence. The local pair contribution is then defined as
\[
\pi_c(i,j) = w_{ij} \cdot \ell_c(i,j).
\]

To prevent long chunks or dense semantic regions from dominating the score through many weak pairs, LiteSemRAG only averages the top-$K$ pair contributions in each chunk:
\[
\mathrm{PairBoost}(c)
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_K(c)|}
\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{P}_K(c)}
\pi_c(i,j),
\]
where $\mathcal{P}_K(c)$ contains the highest-scoring valid semantic-node pairs in chunk $c$. If no valid pair is available, $\mathrm{PairBoost}(c)=0$.

\subsubsection{Final Chunk Ranking}

The final retrieval score combines direct semantic evidence with bounded co-occurrence support:
\[
\mathrm{Score}(c)
=
\mathrm{Base}(c)
\cdot
\left(1+\lambda \cdot \mathrm{PairBoost}(c)\right),
\]
where $\lambda$ controls the strength of the co-occurrence boost. This multiplicative form treats direct semantic evidence as the primary retrieval signal and uses co-occurrence as supporting evidence. A chunk with a strong individual semantic match can still rank highly through $\mathrm{Base}(c)$, while a chunk that jointly supports several query-relevant meanings receives an additional boost.

This unified ranking avoids separating pair-supported and single-node evidence into different retrieval pools. All candidate chunks are ranked by the same score, and co-occurrence improves the rank only when it is locally verified inside the candidate chunk.

\subsection{Extensibility of the Index Graph}
In the current framework, we restrict retrieval to direct semantic evidence and bounded co-occurrence-aware ranking to maintain computational efficiency and robustness. However, the underlying structure of LiteSemRAG's heterogeneous semantic index graph naturally supports more complex graph-based reasoning operations. The heterogeneous graph structure connects documents, chunks, semantic nodes, and token nodes in a bidirectional manner. This design enables flexible traversal across different elements and levels, allowing more advanced operations, including multi-hop semantic expansion, constrained subgraph extraction, and path-based relevance scoring. Therefore, LiteSemRAG should be viewed not only as a retrieval algorithm but also as a structured semantic indexing framework. The index graph provides a foundation for future extensions that incorporate deeper graph traversal strategies or reasoning mechanisms without requiring LLM-based graph construction.

\section{Experiments}
We evaluate LiteSemRAG against representative graph-based RAG systems in terms of retrieval effectiveness and computational efficiency. The experiments are designed to answer the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Does LiteSemRAG achieve competitive retrieval performance compared with LLM-based graph RAG systems?
    \item How does LiteSemRAG compare with existing graph RAG systems in indexing and query efficiency?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets}

We conduct experiments on three widely used retrieval benchmarks from BEIR \cite{thakur2021beir}: \textbf{HotpotQA} \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, a multi-hop question answering dataset that requires retrieving evidence from multiple documents; \textbf{SciFact} \cite{Wadden2020FactOF}, a scientific fact verification dataset focused on evidence retrieval; and \textbf{FEVER} \cite{thorne2018fever}, a large-scale fact verification dataset in which claims must be supported or refuted using evidence retrieved from Wikipedia. For all datasets, documents are split into fixed-length chunks using the same preprocessing strategy across all compared methods to ensure a fair comparison.

\subsubsection{Baselines and Implementation Details}

We compare LiteSemRAG with \textbf{NoLLMRAG} and two representative LLM-based graph RAG systems: \textbf{GraphRAG} and \textbf{LightRAG}. Since GraphRAG and LightRAG require LLMs during indexing and querying, we evaluate them under two LLM settings: \textbf{GPT-4o-mini}, accessed through an API, and \textbf{Llama3-8B-Instruct}, deployed locally. The two LLM-based RAG systems use identical LLM configurations within each setting.
All baseline systems use \textbf{text-embedding-3-large} as the text embedding encoder. In contrast, LiteSemRAG uses \textbf{DeBERTa-v3-large} as its token-level text encoder. This difference is necessary because LiteSemRAG requires contextual token-level embeddings for span-level semantic node construction, whereas the compared RAG systems mainly rely on sentence-level or chunk-level embeddings. During semantic discovery, LiteSemRAG further uses a cross-encoder to support candidate semantic judgment. In our experiments, we use \textbf{nli-deberta-v3-large} for candidate-margin veto and fallback assignment of unresolved occurrences during indexing. We use \textbf{Wikidata} as the external knowledge source for deriving candidate semantic meanings. LiteSemRAG uses LLM assistance only for semantic description selection and anchor labeling during semantic-label preparation; its retrieval stage does not rely on any generative LLM. For LiteSemRAG, the GPT and Llama variants differ only in the indexing-time LLM used for semantic description selection and anchor labeling; the encoder, cross-encoder, graph construction, and query algorithm are otherwise identical. All experiments are conducted on a server equipped with two AMD EPYC 9354 CPUs and one AMD MI210 GPU.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Scope}
LiteSemRAG is designed as a retrieval framework rather than an end-to-end RAG system. Therefore, we evaluate only the quality of retrieved chunks. For fair comparison, we directly measure retrieval effectiveness using Recall@10 and MRR@10, where retrieved chunks are judged relevant according to whether their source documents match the benchmark gold documents. We do not compare final answer quality obtained by feeding retrieved chunks into LLMs, since such evaluation would introduce additional variability from generation models and obscure differences in retrieval performance. This design isolates the retrieval component and ensures that performance differences reflect retrieval capability rather than downstream LLM reasoning or generation quality.
\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We evaluate retrieval quality by using \emph{Recall@10} and \emph{MRR@10}. For each query $q$ in a query dataset $Q$, let $R_q^{(10)}$ denote the ranked top-10 retrieved chunks, and let $D_q^\ast$ denote the set of gold (ground-truth) documents associated with $q$. Since the benchmark annotations provide gold documents rather than gold chunks, we evaluate chunk retrieval under document-level relevance: a retrieved chunk is considered relevant if its source document is one of the gold documents for the query. Let $\mathrm{doc}(c)$ denote the source document of a retrieved chunk $c$, and let $\hat{D}_q^{(10)}=\{\mathrm{doc}(c)\mid c\in R_q^{(10)}\}$ denote the set of source documents covered by the top-10 retrieved chunks. In HotpotQA dataset, each query has exactly two gold documents. In SciFact and FEVER datasets, each query has between one and five gold documents. We compute Recall@10 using a micro-averaged formulation:
\[
\text{Recall@10} =
\frac{
\sum_{q \in Q} | \hat{D}_q^{(10)} \cap D_q^\ast |
}{
\sum_{q \in Q} | D_q^\ast |
}.
\]
This measures the proportion of gold documents that are successfully covered by the top-10 retrieved chunks across the entire dataset; if multiple retrieved chunks come from the same gold document, that document is counted only once for Recall@10. For each query $q$ in a query dataset $Q$, let $\text{rank}_q$ denote the rank position of the first retrieved chunk $c \in R_q^{(10)}$ such that $\mathrm{doc}(c)\in D_q^\ast$. If no retrieved chunk from a gold document appears in the top 10, the reciprocal rank is defined as 0; otherwise, $\text{RR@10}(q)=1/\text{rank}_q$. The Mean Reciprocal Rank at 10 is computed as $\text{MRR@10}=|Q|^{-1}\sum_{q \in Q}\text{RR@10}(q)$. Recall@10 measures how completely the required evidence documents are covered by retrieved chunks, while MRR@10 evaluates how early at least one relevant document appears in the ranked chunk list. In addition, we report four efficiency metrics: Average Indexing Time per document (AIT), Average Query Time (AQT), average online generative-LLM Index Token Consumption per document (ITC), and average online generative-LLM Query Token Consumption (QTC). These metrics quantify both computational and cost efficiency.

\subsection{Ablation Study on Retrieval Strategy}
To evaluate the contribution of the semantic co-occurrence retrieval strategy, we conduct an ablation study using a broad search and reranking retrieval variant. In this setting, we first retrieve all semantic nodes matched by the query. All chunks connected to these semantic nodes are then collected, and a \textbf{jina-reranker-v3} reranker is applied to rank the candidate chunks. If the number of candidate chunks becomes extremely large, we randomly sample 100 chunks as reranking candidates to keep the reranking cost bounded. The top-10 chunks are selected as the final results. This ablation removes the bounded semantic co-occurrence scoring mechanism while preserving the same semantic index graph and matching process. In particular, both methods use the identical index graph constructed during the indexing stage and rely on the same token-level text encoder to generate contextual embeddings. The only difference lies in the retrieval strategy applied at query time. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_results} compares the standard LiteSemRAG and the broad search and reranking variant in terms of Recall@10 (R@10), MRR@10 (M@10), and Average Query Time (AQT). Since both methods operate on the same index graph, the comparison isolates the effect of the retrieval strategy itself, although the absolute scores of the broad reranking variant are also affected by the broad candidate set and the 100-candidate sampling cap. The results show that the semantic index alone is insufficient; group-aware semantic matching and locally verified co-occurrence scoring are critical for turning matched semantic nodes into a high-quality ranked chunk list.

\subsection{Experiments on Anchor-guided Semantic Assignment Algorithm}
To further evaluate the proposed anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm, we construct a dedicated polysemy evaluation set from HotpotQA. For each potentially polysemous span, we collect 100 contextual occurrences and use LLM assistance to generate candidate semantic descriptions and gold labels. This dataset is designed to directly measure whether the proposed method can assign the correct semantic meaning to each span occurrence in context. We evaluate the anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm on this dataset. The algorithm propagates semantic labels from explicitly judged anchors over an occurrence embedding graph, while using a cross-encoder to provide candidate-level semantic evidence, veto unreliable propagation decisions, and resolve remaining uncertain cases. We compare it with two baselines: (1) a cross-encoder labeling baseline, which independently assigns each occurrence to the highest-scoring candidate meaning, and (2) a mutual-kNN propagation baseline without cross-encoder verification or fallback. In this experiment, the anchor-guided semantic assignment algorithm selects 10\% of the occurrences of each span as semantic anchors. The gold labels and anchor labels are produced independently, and the tested algorithm cannot see gold labels beyond the selected anchors. Accuracy is first computed separately for each evaluated span over its 100 occurrences, and Table~\ref{tab:span_ablation} reports the mean and standard deviation across evaluated spans. For the full algorithm, the reported accuracy includes the selected anchor occurrences because LLM-based anchor labeling is part of the end-to-end semantic assignment process.

The results show that the proposed algorithm substantially outperforms both independent cross-encoder labeling and mutual-kNN-only label propagation, while requiring LLM labels for only a small portion of anchor occurrences. The mutual-kNN propagation accuracy further suggests that the occurrence embedding graph can effectively transfer semantic labels to nearby contexts, rather than merely acting as a preliminary step before cross-encoder fallback. However, the higher standard deviation of mutual-kNN-only propagation compared with the full algorithm reflects the difficulty of span-level polysemy across different surface lexical units. Some units form compact and well-separated contextual regions, whereas others contain rare senses or gradual semantic transitions that are difficult to propagate reliably. The even larger standard deviation of cross-encoder labeling suggests that candidate-level scoring alone is less stable across lexical units. These results support the design of combining graph propagation with cross-encoder verification and fallback. Propagation efficiently handles locally coherent cases, while the verifier prevents uncertain, rare, or boundary cases from being assigned unreliable propagated labels.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented LiteSemRAG, a lightweight semantic-aware graph retrieval framework that reduces the recurring dependence of graph RAG systems on generative LLMs. The central idea is to construct a heterogeneous graph in which surface lexical units, context-dependent semantic nodes, and chunks remain distinct, so retrieval can preserve phrase-level lexical evidence while modeling polysemy through contextual span meanings. LiteSemRAG further uses anchor-guided semantic assignment to build inspectable semantic nodes from sparse labeled anchors, mutual-kNN propagation, cross-encoder veto, and fallback assignment, and then retrieves chunks with a semantic co-occurrence score that combines direct matches with bounded local support. Across HotpotQA, SciFact, and FEVER, the results show that LiteSemRAG provides competitive or stronger retrieval performance on most reported metrics than LLM-based graph RAG baselines, achieves the lowest indexing and query latency among comparable semantic graph retrieval systems, and removes generative-LLM token use from the query stage. These findings suggest that graph RAG can retain contextual semantic structure without making LLM calls the main mechanism for indexing and retrieval.
